# Introduction and Before You Go

## Who this book is for

This pocket itinerary is for travelers who want the best of the Monterey coast and Big Sur in three unhurried days. You'll need a car, comfortable shoes and a taste for scenic driving. You don't need to be an expert hiker.

## The plan at a glance

- **Day 1, Monterey:** Cannery Row, the aquarium and 17-Mile Drive.
- **Day 2, Carmel and Point Lobos:** Carmel-by-the-Sea in the morning, then Point Lobos.
- **Day 3, Big Sur:** Bixby Bridge, Pfeiffer Beach, lunch in Big Sur and McWay Falls.

Each day is short enough to leave room for slow mornings, long views and a second look at anything you love.

## Getting there

San Francisco to Monterey is about 113–120 miles. Allow about 2–2.5 hours via US-101, CA-156 and CA-1, or about 2.5–3 hours if you stay on Highway 1 the whole way. Traffic varies.

## When to go

September–October is clearest, with fewer crowds. Summer is the busiest season, with frequent morning fog. November–March is the rainy season, when slides are likelier. Bring layers whatever the month, because the coast can turn cool and breezy quickly.

## Highway 1 road status

Slides and fires close stretches of Highway 1. In 2026, Regent's Slide closed a section early in the year, and fire-support closures followed in August and September. Always check Caltrans QuickMap (quickmap.dot.ca.gov) before you go, and check it again the morning you drive south.

## Services in Big Sur

Cell service in Big Sur is very limited. Gas stations are few and pricey, so fill up in Carmel or Monterey. Download offline maps before you leave. Carry water and a few snacks too, so a slow stretch of road doesn't spoil your day.

## Before you set off

- Check QuickMap for closures.
- Fill the tank in Monterey or Carmel.
- Download offline maps.
- Pack layers, water and comfortable shoes.

Prices, hours and road status change, so check before you go.


# Day 1: Monterey

![](/Users/thomas/workspaces/the-trip-now-www/books/big-sur/images/day1-cannery-row.jpg)

Start your trip with a day that moves from waterfront history to aquarium wonders to a scenic coastal drive. Wear comfortable shoes and bring a layer, since the bay breeze can turn cool.

## Cannery Row in the Morning

Begin on Cannery Row while the day is still fresh. Sardine canning started here in 1902, and the canneries had closed by the mid-1960s. Wander the street at an easy pace, then walk to the aquarium at the other end.

> **Did you know?** The street was called Ocean View Avenue until 1958, when it was renamed after John Steinbeck's 1945 novel "Cannery Row".

## Monterey Bay Aquarium

The Monterey Bay Aquarium sits right on Cannery Row. It opened in 1984 on the old Hovden sardine cannery site. An adult ticket costs about $65, and you should book ahead. It is usually open about 10:00–17:00.

> **Did you know?** The cannery's boiler house and smokestacks were kept. The Kelp Forest tank is about 28 ft tall and was one of the first to keep a living kelp forest.

## 17-Mile Drive in the Afternoon

After the aquarium, head for Pebble Beach and 17-Mile Drive. It is a private toll road, and you pay about $12–12.50 per car at the gate. The road is open sunrise to sunset. Take your time, pull over where it is safe, and keep your camera handy.

> **Did you know?** The Lone Cypress on its rock above the surf has been the Pebble Beach Company's logo since about 1919. Photos for yourself are fine, but commercial use of its image isn't.

## Where to stay

- **HI Monterey Hostel**, 778 Hawthorne St, a block from Cannery Row: budget, with private rooms or dorms.
- **Asilomar Hotel & Conference Grounds**, Pacific Grove: mid-range, with historic lodge buildings in the dunes on a state-park beach.
- **Monterey Plaza Hotel & Spa**, 400 Cannery Row: a splurge, built over the water.

## Where to eat

- **Old Fisherman's Grotto**, on the wharf since 1950: clam chowder in a sourdough bowl, $$.

Prices, hours and road status change, so check before you go.

# Day 2: Carmel-by-the-Sea and Point Lobos

![](/Users/thomas/workspaces/the-trip-now-www/books/big-sur/images/day2-carmel.jpg)

Today is a slower day. You'll stroll through a storybook village in the morning, then spend the afternoon on the coast just to the south. Wear comfortable shoes and bring a layer.

## Morning in Carmel

Leave the car parked and explore on foot. Houses in Carmel have names instead of street numbers, so you'll be looking for names rather than numbers.

## The Storybook Cottages

Hugh Comstock built his fairy-tale cottages between 1924 and 1928. Start with Hansel and Gretel on Torres Street, between 5th and 6th. Then wander the nearby lanes and see how many more you can spot.

> **Did you know?** Comstock built the first storybook cottage for his wife's handmade dolls. Clint Eastwood was the town's mayor from 1986 to 1988.

## Carmel Beach

Finish the morning at Carmel Beach, which welcomes dogs. Bring yours, or just enjoy watching everyone else's. Layers help here, since coastal mornings can be cool.

## Afternoon at Point Lobos

Point Lobos State Natural Reserve lies just south of Carmel. It is day use only, and it's open from about 8:00 to sunset, which is about 19:00 in summer. Day use costs about $10 per car.

The lot fills early, so arrive early or park on Highway 1 and walk in. For an afternoon visit, plan on the walk-in option. No vehicles over 21 ft are allowed inside. Carry water and keep a layer handy as you explore.

> **Did you know?** The little Whalers Cabin was built in the 1850s by Chinese fishermen. Portuguese shore whalers used the cove from 1862. Today the cabin is a small museum.

## Where to stay

- **Cypress Inn:** mid–upper; in the village; Doris Day's hotel; pet-friendly.
- **Mission Ranch:** mid; Clint Eastwood's restored ranch, with sheep in the meadow and views of Point Lobos.

## Where to eat

- **Hog's Breath Inn:** founded by Clint Eastwood in 1970; courtyard with fire pits; steak or chowder; $$–$$$.
- **Mission Ranch restaurant:** sunset dinner with the meadow and Point Lobos view; piano bar; $$$.

Prices, hours and road status change, so check before you go.

# Day 3: Big Sur

![](/Users/thomas/workspaces/the-trip-now-www/books/big-sur/images/day3-bixby.jpg)

Today you drive south on Highway 1 along the Big Sur coast. Plan for slow miles and frequent stops.

## Before you set out

Slides and fires close stretches of Highway 1. In 2026 these included Regent's Slide early in the year and fire-support closures in August and September. Check Caltrans QuickMap (quickmap.dot.ca.gov) before you go.

Cell service in Big Sur is very limited, so download offline maps. Gas stations are few and pricey, so fill up in Carmel or Monterey. Bring water, layers and comfortable shoes.

## Bixby Creek Bridge

Your first big photo stop is Bixby Creek Bridge, completed in 1932. It is about 714 ft long and about 260 ft above the creek. Use the small pull-outs, where parking is limited, and never stop on the bridge itself.

> **Did you know?** The bridge opened on Nov 27, 1932, for about $200,000, so the new coast road (Highway 1, finished in 1937) could cross Bixby Creek.

## Pfeiffer Beach

Pfeiffer Beach sits in Los Padres National Forest and is reached by narrow Sycamore Canyon Road, about 2.2 miles long. No RVs or trailers are allowed. Day use is about $15. The lot holds 65 cars and is full on summer weekends, so come early or choose another day.

> **Did you know?** The purple streaks in the sand come from manganese garnet washed down from the hills. The Keyhole Arch glows at sunset around the winter solstice.

## Lunch at Nepenthe

Stop for lunch at Nepenthe, serving Big Sur since 1949. It is known for the Ambrosia Burger, eaten on the cliff terrace, and prices run $$–$$$.

> **Did you know?** Nepenthe opened on April 24, 1949, and its terrace appears in the 1965 film "The Sandpiper".

## McWay Falls

Finish at McWay Falls in Julia Pfeiffer Burns State Park. The overlook walk is short, about 0.6 mile round trip. Day use is about $10 per car, and the same day's receipt also works at Point Lobos, Pfeiffer Big Sur and Andrew Molera.

> **Did you know?** Above the falls stood the 1940 "Waterfall House" of Lathrop and Helen Hooper Brown. Helen gave the land to the state, asking that the park be named for her neighbour, the pioneer rancher Julia Pfeiffer Burns. The house was torn down in the mid-1960s.

## Where to stay

For anyone spending the night in Big Sur. Call ahead: fires and slides close places at short notice.

- **Big Sur Lodge** (mid): the only lodging inside Pfeiffer Big Sur State Park; books far ahead.
- **Glen Oaks Big Sur** (mid–upper): 1950s adobe motor lodge and cabins in the redwoods.
- **Deetjen's Big Sur Inn** (budget–mid for Big Sur): 1930s inn, no Wi-Fi or cell.
- **Post Ranch Inn** (splurge): cliff-top rooms over the Pacific.

## Where to eat

- **Nepenthe**: the cliff-terrace lunch above, since 1949, known for the Ambrosia Burger; $$–$$$.
- **Big Sur Bakery**: morning pastries and a wood-fired lunch; $–$$; closed Tuesdays.
- **Deetjen's dining room**: breakfast, where eggs Benedict is the classic; $$.

Prices, hours and road status change, so check before you go.

# Practical Information

## Driving times

San Francisco to Monterey is about 113–120 miles. Allow about 2–2.5 hours via US-101, CA-156 and CA-1, or about 2.5–3 hours all on Highway 1. Traffic varies.

## Day-use fees and your shared receipt

- Point Lobos State Natural Reserve: about $10 per car.
- McWay Falls in Julia Pfeiffer Burns State Park: about $10 per car.
- Pfeiffer Beach: day use about $15.

Keep your receipt. The same day's McWay Falls receipt also works at Point Lobos, Pfeiffer Big Sur and Andrew Molera. Plan your stops so one fee covers as many as possible.

## Parking tips

- Point Lobos is day use only. It opens about 08:00 and closes at sunset (about 19:00 in summer). The lot fills early, so arrive early or park on Highway 1 and walk in. No vehicles over 21 ft are allowed inside.
- Pfeiffer Beach is reached by narrow Sycamore Canyon Road, about 2.2 miles long, with no RVs or trailers. The 65-car lot is full on summer weekends, so go early or on a weekday.
- McWay Falls is a short overlook walk of about 0.6 mile round trip, so you won't need much time there.

## Highway 1 closures

Slides and fires close stretches of Highway 1. In 2026 that meant Regent's Slide early in the year and fire-support closures in August and September. Always check Caltrans QuickMap (quickmap.dot.ca.gov) before you go. Check again the morning you drive.

## Cell service and fuel

Cell service in Big Sur is very limited, so download offline maps before you leave. Gas stations are few and pricey, so fill up in Carmel or Monterey. Carry water and layers, too.

Prices, hours and road status change, so check before you go.


# What to Pack

The coast changes quickly, so pack for a few kinds of weather and for long stretches without help.

## Layers for fog and wind

Bring a light base layer, a warm middle layer and a windproof jacket. Summer is the busiest season and has frequent morning fog, so a cold start can turn into a bright afternoon. September–October is the clearest and quietest time to go, but the wind off the water can still chill you at overlooks. If you come between November and March, the rainy season, add a waterproof shell.

## Comfortable walking shoes

You'll be on cliff paths, beach sand and uneven trails. Wear broken-in shoes with good grip, and keep a dry pair in the car.

## Water and snacks

Pack more water than you think you'll need, plus snacks for the road. Stops can be far apart, so having your own food means you can pull over wherever the view is best.

## Maps and power

- **Offline maps:** Cell service in Big Sur is very limited, so download your maps before you leave.
- **A paper map:** It needs no signal or battery, and it makes a good backup.
- **A phone charger:** Bring a car charger and a small power bank.

## Fuel up first

Gas stations in Big Sur are few and pricey, so fill your tank in Carmel or Monterey.

Prices, hours and road status change, so check before you go.
